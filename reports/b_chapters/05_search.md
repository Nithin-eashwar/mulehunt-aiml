# Chapter 5. Tracing stolen money with search

> Real transaction data is legally unavailable; we built a simulator calibrated to NPCI/RBI statistics and validated against the IBM AML benchmark.

Full tables and figures: [`reports/b_02_search.md`](../b_02_search.md), regenerated by `python -m mulehunt.trace`. Every number here comes from `reports/b_tables/search_*.csv`.

## 5.1 Problem formulation

- **State:** `(account, arrival_time)` — which account holds the traced money and since when. The same account reached at two times is two states, because the time decides which onward payments are possible.
- **Initial state (B-D3):** the state right after a flagged victim → tier-1 mule payment lands.
- **Actions and transition:** from `(u, t)`, follow a payment `u → v` at time `t'` with `t < t' ≤ t + 48 h`, giving `(v, t')`. The 48 h window (B-D2) matches Person A's simulator, which caps every hop at 48 h; patient mules wait 24-47 h.
- **Goal test (B-D1):** the account is a known cash-out account (`role == "cashout"`), as if the bank had seen the withdrawal. This is the only ground-truth read inside search, and a test checks that `mulehunt/search.py` never reads `role`, `is_fraud` or `scenario`.
- **Path cost:** hops for the uninformed searches; `−log(risk_prob of the receiver)` (B-D4) for UCS and A\*, so the cheapest trail is the one whose accounts the classifier finds most suspicious overall.

**Why time matters.** In a static graph, a search could follow a payment that happened *before* the stolen money arrived, which cannot be carrying it. With the time rule every trail is strictly increasing in time, so tree search cannot loop. A unit test pins the boundaries: a hop at +0 h is illegal, at exactly +48 h legal.

The graph (`mulehunt/graph.py`) keeps the 1,803,905 edges in numpy arrays sorted by (sender, time), so legal successors are two binary searches. It loads in 17 s with a 917 MB process peak (`graph_load_timing.csv`).

## 5.2 The algorithms

| Algorithm | Frontier | Goal test | Complete | Optimal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BFS | FIFO, graph search | at generation | yes | fewest hops |
| DFS | LIFO, tree search | at generation | yes here (time increases, graph finite) | no |
| DLS (6) | DFS with depth limit | at generation | within the limit | no |
| IDDFS (≤ 8) | DLS with limits 1, 2, … | at generation | yes | fewest hops |
| UCS | priority queue on g | at pop | yes | cheapest |
| A\* | priority queue on g + h | at pop | yes | cheapest (h admissible) |

All run with a budget of 200,000 expansions; a run that hits it is reported as *truncated*, never as "not found". None did.

## 5.3 Results over every start

2,278 victim-payment starts across all 75 rings (`search_comparison.csv`):

| Algorithm | Success (own cash-out) | Mean expanded | Median expanded | Mean peak stored | Mean hops |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BFS | 99.47% | 5.41 | 5 | 28.3 | 2.75 |
| DFS | 99.47% | 13.21 | 3 | 7.96 | 2.75 |
| DLS (6) | 99.47% | 3.41 | 3 | 7.72 | 2.75 |
| IDDFS | 99.47% | 7.97 | 8 | 7.98 | 2.75 |
| UCS | 99.47% | 10.04 | 8 | 26.7 | 2.75 |
| A\*(h1), A\*(h2) | 99.47% | 10.04 | 8 | 26.7 | 2.75 |

Every algorithm traces **all 75 rings** from at least one start. The 12 failing starts (0.5%) are in `job_task` and `loan_app` rings, where a particular victim's payment is not forwarded within 48 h.

The textbook trade-offs show up in the details rather than in the success rate:
- **BFS stores the most** (28 states at peak vs 8 for DFS/IDDFS) and always returns the fewest-hop trail.
- **DFS wanders.** Its median is the best (3 expansions), but its mean is 13.2, pulled up by `loan_app` starts (mean 18.8) where it dives into a busy mule's legitimate branches before backtracking. It is the only algorithm with trail precision below 1 (0.9999).
- **IDDFS** matches BFS's trails with DFS-like memory, paying ~1.5× BFS's expansions for repeating the shallow levels.

The data is cleaner than reality: within 48 h of receiving stolen money, mules rarely make payments that can themselves reach a cash-out in time, so wrong turns die out quickly. That is a limitation of the simulator (5.7), not a property of real UPI.

## 5.4 Search vs the classifier (headline)

For each ring, we compare the mules (tiers 1-3) found on trails with those the random forest flags at its validation-F1 threshold (0.484) (`search_vs_classifier.csv`):

| Scenario | Mules | RF flags | Trails find (all trails ≤ 6 hops) | Found **only** by trails | Genuine accounts on trails |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| fake_kyc | 92 | 32 (0.35) | 92 (1.00) | 60 | 0 |
| job_task | 95 | 54 (0.57) | 95 (1.00) | 41 | 0 |
| digital_arrest | 88 | 65 (0.74) | 88 (1.00) | 23 | 0 |
| loan_app | 85 | 75 (0.88) | 85 (1.00) | 10 | 0 |
| fake_investment | 95 | 83 (0.87) | 95 (1.00) | 12 | 1 |
| **all** | **455** | **309 (0.68)** | **455 (1.00)** | **146** | **1** |

*All trails* collects every account on any time-respecting trail of ≤ 6 hops from the start to a cash-out. With only the single BFS trail per start, trails find 377 mules (0.83), 103 missed by the classifier, and together the two reach 0.905. Fan-out rings (one tier-1 mule paying 3-5 tier-2 mules) are why one shortest trail is not enough.

**This is the headline:** the classifier misses 65% of `fake_kyc` mules (sleepers with normal history), and following the money from one flagged victim payment recovers all of them, at the cost of one genuine account. The B-D1 assumption matters here: the goal set is the true cash-out accounts.

## 5.5 Window sensitivity

| Window | BFS success |
|---|---|
| 12 h | 65.1% |
| 24 h | 82.8% |
| 48 h | 99.5% |

Patient mules (24-47 h) are lost at the shorter windows. Shrinking the window would make search faster but would miss a third of the trails.

## 5.6 A\* and its heuristics

**h1 (plan): `h1(n) = m · d(n)`**, where `d(n)` is the reverse-BFS hop distance to the nearest cash-out ignoring time, and `m` the cheapest edge cost into any account that can still reach a goal. Time rules only remove paths, so the true number of remaining hops is at least `d(n)`, each costing at least `m`: admissible. For an edge `n → n'`, `d(n) ≤ d(n') + 1`, so `h1(n) − h1(n') ≤ m ≤ cost`: consistent.

**h2 (layered): `h2(n) = Σ_{j<d(n)} L_j`** with `L_j` the cheapest entry into an account at distance `j`. One hop lowers `d` by at most 1, so a trail from `n` must enter every layer `d(n)−1, …, 0`; its cost is at least the sum of the cheapest entries: admissible. Consistency follows layer by layer, and since `L_j ≥ m`, `h2 ≥ h1`.

**Measured:** `m = 1.52e-4` (the riskiest account scores 0.99985), and `L_0 … L_5 = 7.8e-4, 1.5e-4, 7.7e-4, 3.9e-3, 0.338, 1.18`. Victim payments start 2-3 hops from a cash-out, where the layers contain ring accounts scored near 1, so both heuristics are almost zero there. **A\*(h1) and A\*(h2) expand exactly as many nodes as UCS** and find identical costs on every start. We report this rather than tune it away: h2 only helps for states 4+ hops from every cash-out.

**What A\* adds is a different trail, not a faster search.** Where both reach the own cash-out, BFS and A\* have the same hop count (2.75) but A\*'s trail has a lower summed `−log(risk)` (0.336 vs 0.437): it picks the most suspicious route, and differs from BFS's on 36% of starts.

## 5.7 Limitations

- **B-D1:** the goal set is perfect knowledge of cash-out accounts. A real bank knows only some; a label-free "sink" goal (money not forwarded, high risk) was planned as optional and is not built.
- **Clean simulator:** sleepers and money collectors exist, but the trails still almost never wander into genuine accounts. Real data would have more genuine branches, more truncation, and lower trail precision.
- **Score windows:** `risk_prob` describes each account inside its own split window. Rings are split-pure, but genuine accounts on a trail may come from any split.
